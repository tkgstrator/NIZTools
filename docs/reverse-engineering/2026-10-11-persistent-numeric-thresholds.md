# Persistent numeric threshold follow-up

The required outcome is a host-chosen threshold written to the keyboard and retained like official calibration, while preserving the stock executable. The physical gesture or choice of implementation is not a requirement. D8 temporary adjustment does not satisfy this outcome. **A reliable persistent numeric setter is not implemented or qualified.**

The exact `66EC(XRGB)BLe;V1.0.48;V1.0;` image was independently reread by two Codex researchers. Its 42,396-byte SHA256 is `b82e11891dfdc138780a81ac635697a7edb0ca1d4e123d6ad38c81a7ba55bf17`. Later research generated synthetic long-record packets and executed them against an offline instruction emulator. **No long-record or numerical T write was sent to hardware.** The coordinator's actual app action was settings read for the separately completed font correction.

## Storage exists; numerical ingress is the gap

Official DD calibration saves the per-cell calibration table T to on-chip flash `0x10e00` through `0x32fa -> 0x8954`. DB saves released baseline B/reference at `0x10c00`/`0x10c44`. Startup reloads them. Therefore the keyboard demonstrably supports independently stored per-key calibration; storage does not inherently require a physical press.

The inspected DD host packet carries no supplied key/table/value. The firmware samples analog input, updates qualifying T cells, saves the whole T block, regenerates normal thresholds R, then replies DE. DD is not a pure save of host-provided bytes. Its flash callers do not check erase/program status, so an acknowledgment alone is not independent restart-persistence verification.

D8 changes R in RAM rather than saved T. DD after D8 measures T again and regenerates R from T and the saved mode; it does not preserve the D8 R table. Under saved coefficients 20/24/28, R is derived from valid T 16..254 or a default sentinel, with rounding and minimum handling. Thus even a future T uploader needs a precise value-conversion contract.

The firmware's DC mode 2 exposes all 66 T bytes in RAM, distinct from DC mode 1's R bytes. A strictly validated read-only client and a matching live keyboard capture are now recorded in [the calibration-value validation](../validation/2026-10-11-calibration-values.md). This helps compare official DD calibration before and after, including changes to other keys. It is not a direct flash-page snapshot or a host-chosen T setter; persistence still requires an independent read after a power cycle.

The ordinary host interfaces, alternative USB control/feature ingress and inspected official DLLs did not establish a numerical T uploader or pure analog save command. The internal IIC writer accesses a separate serial EEPROM used for keymap/RGB/update staging; its addresses do not alias the on-chip calibration flash.

The same question was checked against later official stock images rather than assuming an update would add the missing command. In XRGB V1.1.9 and V1.2.6, the T flash writer is still called directly only by D5 (whole-table initialization) and DD (physical measurement and calibration). RGB V1.5.1 adds D0, but that branch performs whole-device initialization and restart without a numeric T argument; its T writer also has only D5/DD direct callers. Hardware compatibility of those images with this keyboard is unproved, and none supplies a verified selected-key numerical setter. On the pinned V1.0.48 image, the FA–FF path forwards at most 22 bytes to the Bluetooth module, SET_REPORT handles one LED byte, and the F0 direct key-index range ends below RAM T. These are bounds on the inspected paths, not a proof against every indirect or factory-only interface.

## Additional candidate routes checked

An unchecked long-record RAM append path can reach T only after overwriting 3,903 preceding bytes, including device control state, keymap metadata, BLE forwarding state, B and R. The inspected cursor/index handling did not establish a bounded T-only seek or preservation of those bytes. This is a corruption candidate, not a qualified implementation. No such packet was sent to hardware.

### Incomplete F0 plus official DD: offline candidate, not a device feature

In the exact image, F0 long-record continuation copies 53 bytes per report starting at RAM `0x2000018d`. A deliberately incomplete record can leave the serial-EEPROM commit branch uncalled while copying across RAM. For A (physical #31, flat T index 13), 74 reports stage 3,922 bytes with a declared length of 3,923; this reaches T indices 0–18. The general 66-key case needs 75 reports to reach the final T cell. A subsequent DC mode-2 read returns all 66 RAM T values without resetting this F0 counter in the isolated emulator. It could check the intended table **before** DD, but cannot check unrelated RAM or flash. The F0 header changes the keymap index and declared length, not the RAM append base; every increment of its counter copies payload bytes. Reducing the declared length below an existing counter underflows the copy calculation, and a short USB packet is not treated as a safe skip. No shorter F0 seek to T was found.

The offline probe staged B=`0xff` for all cells and a synthetic 66-byte T fixture with one value changed; it did not use live T bytes. Across stubbed 8-bit ADC results 0, 1, 15, 16, 127, 254 and 255, DD called T writer `0x8954` once and did not call the B writer `0x88fc` or instrumented F0 serial-EEPROM writer `0x3334`; all other emulated T cells stayed unchanged. With B=`0xff`, the DD update delta `max(sample - B, 0)` is zero for byte-valued samples. These are **isolated instruction-path results**, not proof of a live write. The emulator resets the stack/register entry for each request and stubs ADC, USB response, delays and flash; it does not schedule the main loop, USB/BLE interrupts or power loss between 74–75 USB reports.

Independent review found a blocking gap for the only available keyboard. Even the shorter A sequence overwrites RAM `0x2000018d..0x200010de`, including runtime state, a BLE forwarding buffer, keymap and derived metadata, B and R. DC2 cannot validate those fields or prevent a background EEPROM write or USB stall. The keyboard also has a BLE battery, so unplugging USB is not guaranteed to reset this volatile state. The full 75-report sequence additionally overwrites six bytes after the 66 T cells; the first two are included in the DD flash write. No read-and-restore path for the crossed RAM was established. **Neither a RAM-only preflight nor the DD write was sent to the sole device.**

The background-write concern has a concrete reachable state dependency: the main loop at `0x8ec0..0x8ec8` checks flag `0x2000097c` and may call `0x87fc`, whose shared-state path at `0x8870` can call serial-EEPROM writer `0x3334`. F0 overwrites the shared state in report 38 and that flag in report 39, long before reaching A's T byte in report 74. Leaving F0 incomplete avoids its own direct EEPROM commit, but does not establish that no main-loop persistence occurs during those reports. Shortening report 74 to stop immediately after A is not supported by the firmware's fixed 53-byte peripheral copy; completing its declared length takes the F0 commit branch.

DD's T writer issues a page erase at `0x10e00`, then programs just 68 bytes: 66 T cells plus two bytes at `0x2000110e..0x2000110f`, the start of a runtime outbound-report ring buffer. The buffer is used by both USB and BLE output paths (`0x2770..0x27ee`, `0x8324..0x85de`) and its bytes can vary with live key output; they are not fixed padding. The NUC123-family interpretation makes that an entire 512-byte page; the exact chip ID has not been read. The writer ignores erase/program failure, and DE can be returned without independent readback. DC2 returns only the 66 T cells, not those two bytes or the rest of the page. The 74-report A case avoids directly overwriting those final two RAM bytes; it still does not solve the crossed-RAM and interrupted-page-write risks. A power failure could lose all saved T values, and there is no verified byte-exact page backup or recovery path on this single device.

The last live read found A T=16 and R=9. This matters to the proposed sensitivity slider: the stock DD path accepts a measured T only from 16 upward, startup normalizes T below 16 to `0xff`, and normal R generation uses `max(9, floor(T × c / 40))` with saved-mode coefficient `c` of 20, 24 or 28. A `0xff` sentinel instead selects mode defaults of 12, 14 or 18. A lower T number therefore does not offer a verified way to make this A key more responsive after restart; 16 already produces the minimum normal R=9. The numerical relationship and a useful value range must be validated before presenting a slider as a fix for A.

The existing updater was also considered as an unchanged stock image plus calibration data, rather than automatically equating every update route with modified executable code. Its application-side checksum validator can accept appended staging bytes, but that does not establish where the bootloader will program them. The cursor is 32-bit, supplied record addresses are 16-bit, and completion length is 16-bit; nonwrapping EEPROM staging holds at most 65,532 image bytes. A simple base-zero linear copy cannot reach calibration flash at `0x10c00`/`0x10e00`. Exact bootloader/LDROM mapping, preservation and recovery behavior are missing, so a calibration trailer is unproved.

A further offline check found that the F2 long-record reader computes the EEPROM record length plus nine in a 16-bit value at `0x5938..0x594c`, then subtracts nine at `0x596e..0x5970` before streaming from RAM at `0x5994..0x59c2`. Near the 16-bit boundary these two lengths differ: the EEPROM-to-RAM copy can wrap to a short length while the later stream remains long. This does **not** establish a usable snapshot route. Reaching that state requires an abnormal stored EEPROM length; the stream has no proved bound to valid SRAM, may fault before completing, and would operate on live RAM rather than a frozen snapshot. No malformed record or hardware probe was produced.

## MCU and loader follow-up

[A 2020 Atom66 XRGB BLE teardown](https://t--log.com/13748/) shows a red daughterboard with a central square MCU and nearby crystal, but the published [board photograph](https://t--log.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/IMG_2127.jpeg) cannot resolve the MCU marking or identify the adjacent unpopulated pad row. A [2021 owner's teardown](https://hendigi.com/niz-keyboard-battery/) shows a `KB66EC(XRGB).Ble` board label, also without a legible MCU marking. Neither photograph establishes the revision, chip identity, or debug pinout of the connected unit. A 2026-10-12 photograph of the connected Atom66 unit now shows board silkscreen `KB66EC ZJB V1.0`, main MCU top marking `HCN213` / `D213801`, and five unpopulated `P3` pads (one square, four round). The photo does not identify the pad signals or the silicon device ID; no probing or wiring was performed.

The application's `0x5000c000` flash controller, `0x50000100` protected-register unlock and `0x40060000` USB peripheral with SRAM at `0x40060100` match the [Nuvoton NUC123 technical reference manual](https://www.nuvoton.com/resource-files/TRM_NUC123_Series_EN_Rev2.04.pdf). Its 68 KB flash map ends at `0x10fff` and uses 512-byte erase pages; the observed T address `0x10e00` is exactly the last page start. Startup `0x6ec0 -> 0x2c60` reads Config0; if Data Flash enable/variable-size bits need correction, `0x2c7c..0x2c8c` clears them and programs Config1 to `0x10000`, consistent with a 4 KB Data Flash area ending at `0x10fff`. This strongly suggests a NUC123-family 68 KB device. The present board is marked `HCN213`, but its silicon device ID has not been read. In a [Pusan National University study of a different HCN213 keyboard](https://his.pusan.ac.kr/bbs/cse/12607/881869/download.do) (PDF pp.18–19, figures 23–25), OpenOCD identified that part as Nuvoton `NUC123LD4AN`, Device ID `0x00012335`, with a `0x11000`-byte flash bank. This corroborates the family inference but does not prove the exact Atom66 part or its lock state. The application's `0x4fb4` update handoff programs Config0 `0x78bfff38`. Under the NUC123 bit definitions, that value selects LDROM and sets the security-lock bit to zero, which the manual defines as **flash content locked**. [Nuvoton's FAQ](https://www.nuvoton.com/support/technical-support/faq/d3ee2358-04f1-11ea-b113-05daf7eedf03/) says that with this lock enabled, external programmer reads of flash return `0xffffffff` and its whole-chip erase removes the lock. Nuvoton [AN0001](https://www.nuvoton.com/resource-files/AN0001_NuMicro_Cortex-M_Code_Protection_EN_V1.00.pdf) (p.4) additionally distinguishes an internal ISP path that can read flash despite LOCK and can unlock by erasing user configuration; that erase is still destructive to configuration and is not a backup/read-only procedure. This is a conditional interpretation of the handoff value, not a live read of the keyboard's present Config0: the current lock state and external readback availability remain unknown. If locked, merely obtaining a programmer would not provide a byte-exact backup of the current Data Flash. A bootloader that actually exports its internal ISP reads could therefore provide a backup, but neither that capability nor a read command is established for this Atom66.

The update handoff at `0x4fb4` page-erases the configuration region at `0x00300000` and writes back only Config0. Nuvoton's [NUC123 BSP flash driver](https://github.com/OpenNuvoton/NUC123BSP/blob/master/Library/StdDriver/src/fmc.c#L263-L266) warns that user configuration is page-erased and must be backed up before an erase. Config1 is at `0x00300004`; no preservation or rewrite of that word appears in this application-side handoff. Startup only repairs Config1 when Config0's Data Flash bits are set, while the handoff word has both cleared. LDROM might restore Config1, but its exact behavior is unknown. Consequently the effective Data Flash base after entering the updater cannot be assumed from this application image. No update handoff was sent to the keyboard.

Nuvoton publishes [macOS ISP source](https://github.com/OpenNuvoton/NuISPCmdLineTool-macOS) for a *standard* ISP loader in LDROM. Its documented USB discovery looks for Nuvoton VID `0x0416`, while this keyboard currently presents `0x0483:0x502a`; it can update Data Flash but its listed commands do not include a general flash dump. The NIZ LDROM image and its USB identity/protocol have not been obtained. Therefore the existence of Nuvoton's tool does not qualify it for this keyboard, provide a complete page backup, or establish a safe numerical write. No boot-mode transition was attempted.

An offline parse of all twelve locally available official Atom66 EC-BLE RGB update packages found only base-zero application records. Their highest decoded addresses range from `0xa110` to `0xd150`, below the Data Flash base; none includes an extended-address segment for Data Flash or an identified LDROM image. Record checksums passed. This package survey does not establish what an already-installed loader may support.

The exact V1.0.48 update image ends at `0xa59c` (exclusive), so it contains neither the B page at `0x10c00` nor the T page at `0x10e00`. The official Windows utility also distinguishes transfer from installation: its success text says the firmware was downloaded, then instructs the user to keep USB connected until three status LEDs flash before unplugging and reconnecting (UTF-16LE string at file offset `0x1b0b90`, referenced by code at `0x415a1c`). The supplied manual describes only the normal connected-utility update sequence, and the updater DLL sends the handoff command `3A` through the already-open HID device. No independent loader-entry or recovery procedure was identified. A successful host transfer therefore cannot be used as evidence that flash programming finished or that a board with a failed application can be recovered.

The [official NUC123 ISP HID sample](https://github.com/OpenNuvoton/NUC123BSP/blob/master/SampleCode/ISP/ISP_HID/isp_user.c) makes the backup gap more concrete: its Data Flash update command first erases the **entire Data Flash region**, disregarding the supplied start-address field, and its command switch has no pre-write Data Flash dump. This describes the sample, **not** the unobserved NIZ LDROM. DC mode 0 exposes 66 RAM baseline B bytes and a reference, and DC mode 2 exposes 66 RAM T bytes, but startup can normalize those values; neither reads the extra two bytes that the B/T writers program, the other Data Flash pages, or Config0/1 byte-for-byte. They therefore cannot serve as a complete image for an erase-and-restore. An updater route needs the exact NIZ loader/MCU and a byte-exact backup of affected flash/configuration. An application-side F0/DD route instead needs proof that its crossed RAM cannot trigger unrelated writes or lose communication, plus a recovery plan for T-page erase/program failure. A recoverable same-model board would then need to verify that only the selected T byte changes after a cold restart, with B, other T cells, configuration, keymap and lighting intact.

These results bound the inspected paths; they do not prove that every hidden factory/module/debug/programmer path is impossible. Further qualification needs a positively identified numerical ingress or exact bootloader/storage evidence before a write can be presented as successful and safe for the other stored data.

## Next evidence gate for the pictured board

The front-side photograph establishes a candidate five-pad `P3` header, but not its electrical pinout. A rear-side photograph could reveal routing; any continuity measurement would first require the board to be unpowered, including its BLE battery. Neither photograph alone can establish the MCU's live device ID, Config0 lock state, or flash contents. No pad should be connected to a programmer by assuming a standard pin order.

If hardware access becomes available, the first useful session is identification/readback only: establish the P3 signals against the MCU pins, read Device ID and Config0/1, and determine whether the exact T page and loader can be dumped byte-for-byte **without** erase or boot-configuration changes. If external readback is locked, investigate an existing internal ISP read path separately; unlocking or entering the present update handoff is not a read-only substitute. A persistent numerical writer and slider need a verified complete backup, an isolated selected-byte update, independent readback after cold restart, and recovery from interrupted page programming. Until those gates pass, the application exposes physical DD calibration with the documented possible changes to other keys and temporary D8 all-key adjustment, but no arbitrary persistent numerical setter.
